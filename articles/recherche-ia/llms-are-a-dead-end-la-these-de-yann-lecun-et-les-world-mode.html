“LLMs Are a Dead End”: What Yann LeCun Is Really Arguing for Instead

Mehmet Özel

This article breaks down what LeCun’s actually saying, why he insists on self‑supervised learning, and what kind of systems he thinks we should build instead of just scaling LLMs.

Large language models are winning the short‑term game: chatbots, copilots, content mills, productivity tools everywhere. But Yann LeCun thinks this entire direction hits a hard wall if the goal is human‑level intelligence.​

This is not a casual hot take. It’s a research agenda: self‑supervised world models (JEPA‑style architectures) trained on the physical world, not just internet text.

What LeCun Thinks Is Wrong With LLMs

LeCun’s core claim is brutal and very specific: LLMs are useful, but they are not a path to human‑level intelligence or “superintelligence.”

From the interview and his recent talks, his critique clusters around four missing capabilities:

  • No understanding of the physical world
    LLMs are trained purely on text, which is a thin, discrete shadow of reality. They never see videos, forces, or objects; just symbol sequences.​​
    He stresses that language is actually simpler than the physical world because it’s discrete and finite (vocabulary, tokens). Predicting the next token is solvable; predicting the next video frame in all its continuous detail is not.​
  • No persistent, usable memory
    Today’s LLMs operate with a limited context window and no robust, structured, long‑term memory of their own interactions or a coherent model of the environment.​​
  • Primitive reasoning and planning
    Current models “reason” by sampling many candidate token sequences and then selecting the one that looks best according to another network. That’s basically search over outputs (token space), not search inside an internal model of the world.​​
    Humans reason in latent space: you rotate a cube in your mind, you plan multi‑step trips with hierarchical subgoals, you don’t brute‑force all possible action sequences.​
  • No grounded common sense
    A cat understands gravity better than any current system: it can jump, land, plan routes in a cluttered environment. We still can’t build robots that reliably match that competence.​​

This leads to his “dead end” conclusion: if you keep scaling this architecture (auto‑regressive, decoder‑only LLMs trained on internet text), you get better chatbots but you don’t get general intelligence that can act, plan, and learn in the real world.​

Why He Bets on Self‑Supervised Learning

LeCun divides machine learning into three paradigms and then argues that self‑supervised learning is the only one that scales to animal‑ and human‑level intelligence.​​

The three paradigms (his framing)

Supervised learning

  • You show an input x and a label y, e.g. image → “cat”.
  • The model adjusts parameters to match the label.
  • Great for narrow tasks, terrible data efficiency and not how babies learn.​

Reinforcement learning

  • You give only scalar feedback (reward/punishment).
  • Works for games where you can simulate millions of episodes (Go, chess, Atari).
  • Completely impractical for driving, robotics, or complex real‑world skill acquisition because you’d need catastrophic numbers of failures.​​

Self‑supervised learning

  • You hide parts of the input and train the model to predict them.
  • In NLP: mask or chop off words, predict them; that’s how LLMs are trained.​
  • Crucially, this does not require labels; it uses the raw structure of data itself as the signal.​​

So why is LeCun so obsessed with self‑supervision?

  • It’s the only paradigm with enough signal to match the data stream animals get (24/7 sensory input).​​
  • It allows building world models internal representations that encode how the environment behaves, not just how words follow each other.​
  • It matches how infants learn: by predicting what happens next in sensory streams (vision, proprioception, sound), not from labels.​

He openly admits he arrived late to this view and considers that a major regret: he stayed focused on supervised learning too long, and only in the mid‑2000s fully shifted to unsupervised / self‑supervised approaches.​

World Models and JEPA: The Alternative Architecture

LeCun’s positive proposal is not “LLMs suck, full stop.” It’s: LLMs are one layer, but the core of intelligence must be world models trained with self‑supervision, using architectures like JEPA.​

What is JEPA in his vision?

JEPA stands for Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. It’s more of a macro‑architecture than a single model: you arrange encoders and predictors that operate in representation space instead of pixel/token space.​​

Key ideas:

  • Learn an abstract representation z=f(x) of sensory input (image, video, audio).​
  • Predict future or missing representations z′ from current context, instead of predicting raw pixels or tokens directly.​​
  • By predicting in this latent space, you can ignore irrelevant low‑level details (exact texture, noise) and focus on semantics (object identity, position, dynamics).​

Meta’s I‑JEPA is the first big public instantiation of this idea:

  • It predicts masked regions of an image in representation space, not pixel space.
  • It learns semantic, object‑centric features that transfer well to downstream tasks and does so more efficiently than many generative pixel‑space methods.​

Newer JEPA‑style work even extends this to language, e.g. Semantic Tube Prediction, which constrains hidden trajectories in an LLM to follow smooth “semantic tubes” and can match baseline accuracy with up to 16× less data.​

Why world models matter

LeCun’s world model story ties back to several points he repeats:

  • The world is its own best training signal. Rich continuous streams like video contain orders of magnitude more information than language.​
  • A child’s visual system in the first four years receives roughly the same information volume (via the optic nerve) as a large LLM consumes in text tokens and uses it to learn intuitive physics, object permanence, social cues, etc.​
  • If you want robots or agents that can plan, manipulate, and generalize like animals, you need models that understand these dynamics, not just label datasets or next‑token patterns.​

In other words: LLMs learn statistics of words; world models learn statistics of reality.

“LLMs Are a Dead End” in Context

It’s easy to misread LeCun as saying “LLMs are useless.” He doesn’t. He explicitly acknowledges:

  • LLMs are astonishingly effective at language understanding and generation.​​
  • Self‑supervised learning on text is a huge breakthrough, and LLMs are already using that paradigm.​​
  • LLMs will power assistants, glasses, productivity tools, and billions of daily interactions.​

His actual “dead end” argument is narrower:

LLMs, as auto‑regressive text predictors with no grounded world model, cannot scale to human‑level intelligence no matter how big you make them.​

Three pillars of that claim:

  1. Scaling text alone saturates
    There is a finite amount of high‑quality text; scaling laws assume more data, but we are already near the limit of public internet text.​
  2. Text doesn’t contain enough structure about the physical world
    Many aspects of reality are rarely or never described in language, yet they are core to our intelligence (sensorimotor skills, continuous control, micro‑dynamics).​
  3. Architecture mismatch
    Auto‑regressive token generators do not naturally support internal simulation, hierarchical planning, or search in latent mental models. They bolt these on with hacks (tool calls, external memory, planning loops), but the core model is still just a sophisticated text next‑token engine.​

So the dead‑end warning is strategic: he fears the “LLM boom” is sucking all the money and talent into an approach that won’t deliver AGI, delaying investment in world models and self‑supervised physical AI.

What This Looks Like in Practice

If you’re building systems today, what does a LeCun‑style, self‑supervised world‑model‑first stack actually look like?

Think in terms of three layers:

  1. Perception world model (self‑supervised, JEPA‑style)
  • Inputs: raw sensory streams video, audio, proprioception, other sensors.
  • Objective: predict masked or future latent states; learn object‑centric, dynamical representations.
  • Output: latent state zt that captures “what’s going on” in a compact, predictive form.

2. Planner / controller over latent space

  • Inputs: current state zt, goal g.
  • Objective: search over sequences of latent actions or states to reach the goal, using the world model for rollouts and value estimation.​​

3. Interface layer (language, UI, tools)

  • LLMs, structured APIs, and symbolic modules that translate between human requests and goals in the planner, and verbalize plans or explanations back to users.​

A minimal JEPA‑style world model skeleton (pseudo‑PyTorch)

This is not LeCun’s actual code, but a simplified template that captures the core pattern:

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# 1) Encoders map raw input to latent space
class VisionEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=1024):
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = SomeConvOrViTBackbone(dim)
    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (B, C, H, W) video frame or image
        return self.backbone(x)  # (B, dim, h’, w’)

# 2) JEPA predictor: predict future/masked latent from context latent
class JEPAPredictor(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=1024):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = SomeTransformerOrUNet(dim)
    def forward(self, z_context, mask):
        # z_context: latent features, mask: where to predict
        return self.net(z_context, mask)  # predicted z at masked locations

# 3) Training step: self-supervised prediction in latent space
def jepa_loss(encoder, predictor, x, mask):
    # x: batch of images/frames
    z = encoder(x)                       # full latent
    z_context = z * (1 - mask)           # hide masked parts
    z_target = z.detach()                # stop gradient
    z_pred = predictor(z_context, mask)  # predict masked latents
    return ((z_pred - z_target) ** 2 * mask).mean()

# 4) Later: add a planner acting in latent space
class LatentPlanner(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=1024):
        super().__init__()
        self.policy = SomeTransformerPolicy(dim)
    def forward(self, z, goal):
        # returns latent action or desired next latent state
        return self.policy(torch.cat([z, goal], dim=-1))

Then you can wire this into a robotics or agent pipeline:

  • Stream video into VisionEncoder to maintain a latent state of the environment.
  • Use LatentPlanner + a value‑guided planning algorithm (e.g. value‑guided JEPA planning) to choose actions that maximize expected reward while simulating consequences in latent space.​
  • Use an LLM only at the top to parse human instructions (“clean the kitchen”) into goal representations and to narrate what the system is doing.

Recent work shows that JEPA‑style models can support such value‑guided action planning, using learned latent dynamics to plan sequences of actions more efficiently than pixel‑space RL.​

Trade‑offs, Limits, and Open Problems

LeCun’s agenda is ambitious and comes with real challenges.

What breaks or is still unclear

  • World models are hard to train at scale
    Learning robust predictive models over high‑dimensional, multi‑modal sensory streams is significantly harder than predicting text tokens. Stability, coverage, and evaluation are active research problems.
  • Data collection is expensive and messy
    You can scrape internet text cheaply; collecting diverse, high‑quality video, robot sensor logs, and interaction traces is expensive and politically sensitive (privacy, surveillance, regulation).​​
  • Benchmarks and metrics are immature
    For LLMs, you have benchmarks (MMLU, coding tasks, etc.). For world models, what’s the metric? Long‑horizon prediction error? Downstream control tasks? Transfer to robotics? Still an open question.
  • LLMs keep improving faster than expected
    The “LLMs are a dead end” narrative might be partially wrong if architectural hybrids emerge: JEPA‑style latent modeling plus strong LLMs plus large‑scale tool use and external memory. Some recent work already pushes in this direction.
  • Biological plausibility vs. engineering pragmatism
    LeCun cares about brain‑inspired algorithms (e.g. backprop vs. cortex learning), but for many engineers, the only question is “does it work in production?” That tension may slow adoption of his more “cortical” designs.

Misconceptions to avoid

  • “Self‑supervised learning = LLMs; therefore LeCun is contradicting himself.”
    No. He agrees LLMs use self‑supervision, but argues they apply it to text only and in the wrong output space (tokens, not general world latents).​​
  • “World models mean pure simulation; just train on synthetic data.”
    His emphasis is on grounding in real sensor data: video, audio, robot sensors, industrial telemetry, etc., not just synthetic environments.
  • “Once we have a world model, AGI is solved.”
    He treats world models as a necessary component: you still need planning, memory, value systems, and probably new neural architectures for hierarchical control.​

Why This Matters Now and What to Do This Week

LeCun just raised around €1B for AMI (Advanced Machine Intelligence), a new company explicitly built around this bet: large‑scale, self‑supervised world models trained on non‑text data.​

At the same time, big players are pouring tens to hundreds of billions into infrastructure mostly aimed at inference for LLM‑based assistants, not training fundamentally new architectures.​​

If you’re a practitioner, this shift matters because it changes what to build if you don’t want to just be “another thin wrapper around an LLM.”

Here are concrete next steps you can take this week:

  1. Start thinking in world‑model architectures, not just prompts
  • When designing an “agent”, explicitly separate:
  • Perception / state estimation (possibly JEPA‑style encoders).
  • Planning in latent space.
  • Language interface (LLM) as an outer shell.

2. Experiment with self‑supervised vision/audio models

  • Play with open‑source implementations of I‑JEPA‑like models or other self‑supervised vision backbones (MAE, etc.), and use them as the state representation for small control tasks (e.g. simulated robotics).

3. Prototype a tiny hybrid agent

  • Use an LLM only to:
  • Turn a natural language goal into a structured goal representation.
  • Explain plans and outcomes to the user.
  • Use a learned latent dynamics model (even a toy one) to do internal rollouts and planning.

LLMs are not going away; they’re an incredibly useful interface layer. But if LeCun is even half right, the systems that matter 5–10 years from now will look less like “giant autocomplete” and more like world‑simulating, self‑supervised control stacks with language as just one of several modalities.
