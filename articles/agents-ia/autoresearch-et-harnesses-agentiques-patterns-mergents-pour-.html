Thoughts on agent harnesses
@karpathy
's
post on autoresearch
has sparked enormous interest in agentic harnesses specialized in assisting researchers with the tedious parts of their work, such as hyperparameter tuning, prompt optimization, training monitoring, and so on. While the field is still very much in its infancy, it is becoming clearer by the day that even current-generation language models have the potential to redefine how research is being conducted, and one might already feel left behind if they have yet to find a way to adopt such a workflow for their day-to-day research tasks. In this article, I want to lay down some of my thoughts on emerging patterns for AI-assisted research, as the science of science is a topic that has been occupying more and more of my brain cycles.

Autoresearch

First of all, what is autoresearch? In plain terms, the original implementation referred to a framework in which an AI agent iterated autonomously on the training loop for
nanochat
, a minimal reference implementation for replicating GPT-2. Similar to a human researcher, the agent would modify the code, run a few training steps or epochs, evaluate the resulting artifacts, and decide whether to keep the changes or discard them before beginning the next round of improvements.
It is easy to see how this pattern of an agent iterating toward a research goal could be generalized to optimize for many research outputs. Simply put, if you can define a metric or an evaluation signal that indicates whether the system improved, you can begin to automate parts of the corresponding research loop. Sounds familiar? That's because this is the same premise that
DSPy
has been operating on for the past two and a half years - define your inputs (code, research proposal, etc) and your performance metrics, and let the LLM hill climb that metric to produce the desired outputs (research report, model weights, and so on).
So the question becomes: why now? For many people, Opus 4.5 signified an inflection point in the agentic capabilities of LLMs, prompting everyone to reconsider what these systems can do autonomously that they could not do before. It turns out that a very capable coding model, when paired with an equally capable harness, is already able to take on many of the tedious tasks that researchers encounter in their daily workflows.

An agentic system for research

For ML research, we can boil it down to a system with the following agents:
  • A planner, which defines the research workflow, with steps such as a literature survey, experiment setup, and ablations.
  • A researcher, with access to the scientific literature, the web, and other research tools and resources.
  • A set of workers, which convert an idea to code, execute it, and debug it.
  • A reviewer, that checks the artifacts at each stage and submits feedback.
  • A writer, which produces digests and progress reports, as well as the final research output and/or experiment log.
The difficult part is tying all of this together in a way that makes cross-agent communication effective, while also providing sufficient context and tooling for the agents to carry out their tasks.

If you've used any of the current generation of LLMs, and particularly strong coding-oriented models like Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.3-Codex / GPT-5.4, you will know that they are often capable of carrying out many of the aforementioned tasks, especially when those tasks are well defined with regard to their inputs and expected outputs. Of course, for exceedingly complex tasks, the success rate will be lower, but typically a strict set of requirements and the presence of guardrails that force the models to iterate until the conditions are met will keep them running until they complete the task. And this is precisely why the question of the harness is more relevant now than ever.

Building effective harnesses

The way I like to think about harnesses is that they are the implementation layer that defines the primitives that the language model interacts with. Very commonly, these primitives include a file system, where the agent can create and update files such as documents and code; a command interpreter, so the agent can execute scripts and interact with the system; a knowledge and database layer, with either internal assets or external resources from the web; and an orchestration framework that allows it to spawn subagents, delegate work, and track progress.
A good harness, in addition to those base primitives, will allow the user to extend it with minimal friction, with the two dominant methods being
skills
and the
Model Context Protocol
(MCP). Skills, on the one hand, are additional instructions that the model can selectively load when deemed appropriate based on a short description, while MCP servers are commonly used to provide the agent with a set of tools that enable it to interact with both local and online services, such as web search providers, databases, and applications.
The advent of harnesses, in my opinion, will completely change how models are used and evaluated. Perhaps controversially, ARC-AGI-3 has
banned harnesses
from its submissions for the ARC Prize, which is a defensible position if the goal is to benchmark the model itself rather than the system around it, but it is arguably missing the point of how AI will actually be deployed. Nonetheless, strong general-purpose harnesses are likely to soon become how most of humanity experiences AI. This is already happening, with Codex & Claude Code being without a doubt the most striking examples of incredibly effective harnesses, and
Claude Cowork
showing a glimpse of what a broader agentic harness product category might look like. If one were to compare the capabilities of the underlying models with the experience provided within these harnesses, we would arrive at a contrast similar to the one that became apparent when ChatGPT launched.

Self-improving harnesses

Currently, almost all agentic harnesses suffer from a lack of continual learning. Each session largely starts with no context about what has happened before, such as previous attempts to solve a task, successful approaches and failure modes, workarounds that were used, and lessons learned. While you could create a skill that instructs the agent to thoroughly document its findings and consult them in new sessions, there is (a) no standard for this, and (b) a creeping danger of
context rot
from including too much informational bloat.

This optimization task is unquestionably challenging, as it requires balancing correctness, speed, and cost, all while preventing overfitting. Recent work like the

Where this all leads for research

I will focus on the impact of agents in AI research, as this is the field that I am familiar with and can actually speak to with some anecdotal evidence from personal experience. But it would not be much of a stretch to imagine similar effects trickling down into many other sciences sooner or later.

Because so much of AI research is mediated through software, such as experiment code, training infrastructure, evaluation stacks, and analysis tooling, we can already envision how a next-generation coding harness, with all of the features described in this article, could autonomously plan, execute, supervise, and orchestrate large-scale experimental workflows on mission-critical challenges toward AGI. It may be that current harnesses still lack the sophistication or robustness to reliably carry out such tasks, but results like
@METR_Evals
' task-completion time horizon work are at least suggestive of steadily improving autonomous performance on longer software tasks.
Big labs aside, what I find most encouraging is how easy it is for someone to create an environment that's purpose-built for their own research workflows. Even if all you use is Claude Code, Codex, or Cursor, creating skills for tasks that either you or an agent repeatedly end up doing, and connecting those systems to other sources via CLI or MCP tools, is a straightforward path towards turbocharging productivity and reducing boilerplate. For some, that might mean building visualization dashboards; for others, monitoring training curves, or even annotating data and classifying failure modes for the more adventurous. But by actually sitting down and rethinking how to wield these tools, rather than dismissing them as pure hype, the force multiplier will only become more difficult to ignore.
