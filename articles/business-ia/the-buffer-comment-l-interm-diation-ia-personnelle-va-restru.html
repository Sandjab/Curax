The End of the Feed: AI and the New Coherence Layer
The internet does not have a curation problem. It has a coherence problem. Every platform you use has already curated its feed — tuned its algorithm, ranked its posts, decided what rises and what falls. The curation is sophisticated. The result is still static. Because each platform optimizes for its own engagement, and the dozens of feeds you live across are locally coherent and globally incoherent. Together they don't add up to a picture of the world. They add up to noise that has learned to look like signal.
The gap between information production and human processing capacity is structural, and it is not getting smaller. We are not going to read faster. Platforms are not going to optimize for your coherence over their revenue. The only thing that changes the arithmetic is something that absorbs the firehose before you do — reading it all, deciding what matters, and handing you back something you can act on.
Call it the The Buffer.

What the Buffer Actually Does

The Buffer is not a new app. It is an intermediary layer — a persistent intelligence that sits upstream of your attention and does the work of ingestion, synthesis, and prioritization before anything reaches you.
In practice, it reads your emails before you do and tells you which three actually require a response. It monitors a hundred news sources and hands you a five-sentence briefing on the two things that are genuinely relevant to what you're working on. It watches the group chats and flags when someone said something that changes your plans. It scans the research literature and extracts the finding that updates your prior. It sits between you and the world and acts as a cognitive shock absorber — taking the full amplitude of information noise and outputting something close to signal.
The scale of what it's absorbing is worth sitting with.
376 billion emails
are sent globally every day. A 2024 study in Science Advances analyzed 12.7 million tweets from 7,331 users and found that moral outrage is systematically amplified through social learning — platforms don't just reflect anger, they teach it. The average knowledge worker context-switches between apps and documents dozens of times per hour. The firehose is not a metaphor. And no human was ever equipped to process it at native resolution.
This is not summarization. Summarization is a feature. The Buffer is infrastructure. The difference is that summarization is a one-time operation you invoke; the Buffer is a continuous process running in the background of your information life, maintaining a model of what you know, what you need to know, and what is irrelevant noise dressed up as urgency.
The coherence it produces is the real product. Not any individual summary or filtered feed, but the emergent sense that your information environment is legible — that the things happening in the world are connected to each other and to you in ways you can understand and act on.

Where It Lives: The Personal AI Server

One of the most consequential questions about the Buffer is where, physically, it runs — and who controls it.
The answer that matters most is also the simplest: it runs on yours. Not in the sense of a specific product or model, but in the sense of infrastructure that sits on your side of the data relationship rather than a platform's side. That principle — the personal AI server — is more important than any particular implementation. The stack changes monthly. The principle doesn't.
What that stack might look like today is instructive. A new generation of open-weight models — built for reliable, persistent instruction-following with strong reasoning capabilities — can be deployed locally, on commodity hardware, without sending data through a third-party API. You configure one with your priorities and preferences, and it runs indefinitely against your feeds, your inbox, your documents, without ever phoning home. Orchestration frameworks like
OpenClaw
and Hermes bind the pipeline together — ingestion, retrieval, filtering, synthesis, formatting — into something that behaves like a coherent agent rather than a series of disconnected prompts. Products like
OsLetaurus
are already building the consumer layer on top: local and cloud models running together on your machine, under your control.
The specific models and tools in use today will be displaced or unrecognizable within two years. What matters is the architecture: a persistent reasoning layer that runs on your hardware or under your control, holds your context, and processes your information streams before any platform sees your attention. Whether it is an open-weight model running locally or a fine-tuned variant purpose-built for this use case — the specifics are almost beside the point. The point is that the synthesis layer belongs to you.
The other path runs through large model APIs — frontier reasoning models from the major labs, harnessed as the reasoning core of the Buffer rather than the entire stack. Here the Buffer is a harness: a persistent orchestration layer that manages context, routes different categories of content to appropriate models, and assembles a coherent output from distributed calls. The large model provides the reasoning depth; the harness provides the memory, routing, and persona configuration that makes it behave like your Buffer rather than a generic assistant. You give up full data sovereignty, but you gain reasoning capability that current local models cannot match for complex synthesis tasks.
These two architectures — local personal server versus large-model harness — are not mutually exclusive, and the most functional personal Buffers will likely be hybrid: a local orchestration layer handling routine ingestion and filtering, with a cloud model invoked for tasks requiring deeper synthesis. The architecture mirrors how knowledge workers already operate: local tools for most things, specialized resources for hard problems.
What both paths share is the key property that matters: the Buffer is yours, not the platform's. It is your agent, running your preferences, optimizing for your coherence rather than their engagement. The platform still produces the content. But you now control the layer that decides what of it reaches you — and in what form.

The Buffer Inverts How Coherence Is Built

Before the Buffer, coherence was the platform's job. Twitter decided what was trending. Google decided what was relevant. The New York Times decided what led. Individual readers synthesized these judgments into a worldview, but the initial filtering happened at the platform layer — and platform incentives shaped everything downstream.
The Buffer moves the coherence-building function out of the platform and into the personal layer. You no longer receive a pre-filtered world and react to it. You receive raw feeds and your Buffer synthesizes them according to your model of what matters. This is a bigger shift than it sounds. The platform's filter was never neutral — it was shaped by ad revenue, by what made people engage, by what kept them on-site. Your Buffer's filter is shaped by you. The bias changes from commercial to personal. It does not disappear, but it becomes legible and adjustable in a way that no platform algorithm has ever been.
The practical consequence is that two people reading the same hundred sources will produce radically different understandings of events — not because they are biased in the traditional sense, but because their Buffers are configured around different frameworks, different priorities, different definitions of relevance. The shared experience of news dissolves further. What replaces it is a world that is individually coherent but collectively fragmentary — which sounds alarming until you measure it against the alternative. The world we have now is not a shared epistemic commons. It is a set of platform-controlled filter bubbles, designed by engineers optimizing for time-on-site. The Buffer at least makes the filter visible and puts it in your hands.
This is where the standard filter bubble critique quietly inverts. Eli Pariser's argument — that personalization creates ideological echo chambers — assumed the personalization was happening to you, not by you. A platform's algorithm optimizes for engagement, which means it feeds you content that confirms and inflames. A Buffer you configure has no such incentive. Tell it to steelman the counterargument. Tell it to surface perspectives from outside your usual sources. Tell it to flag when a claim you believe is contested by credible evidence. A well-configured Buffer can be the most aggressive intellectual diversity tool ever built — more effective than any editorial policy, because it operates at the synthesis layer rather than the selection layer. The filter bubble is not a property of personalization. It is a property of personalization optimized for someone else's revenue. Remove that incentive and the bubble can become a lens.

What the Buffer Does to Content Creation

Here is what most creators have not reckoned with: if AI intermediates all consumption, the entire logic of what makes content effective inverts.
Right now, content is optimized for human attention — headlines designed to generate clicks, thumbnails designed to generate views, opening sentences designed to prevent scrolling away. The entire production apparatus of modern media is calibrated to the properties of human perception: our susceptibility to novelty, outrage, curiosity gaps, social proof. These are the handles that content grabs to get processed before something else does. The Buffer removes those handles from the equation. Your AI intermediary is not susceptible to a provocative headline. It does not reward clickbait with attention. It reads the article anyway, extracts the signal, and presents you with a sentence. The emotional packaging is stripped before it reaches you.
Density will matter more than drama. The fundamental unit of content value shifts from attention capture to insight yield. A 600-word piece with three genuine insights will surface more reliably through a Buffer than a 3,000-word piece with one insight buried under atmospheric prose. Signal-to-noise ratio becomes the primary quality metric, not engagement rate. This hits explainer journalism hardest — the genre built its entire existence on making complex ideas accessible through entertaining prose. But if the Buffer can compress the idea into a sentence anyway, the entertainment scaffolding stops doing work. What survives is the idea. If you don't have one, neither the prose nor the packaging saves you.
Structural honesty replaces SEO. The first death of search engine optimization came from AI-generated results that bypass the blue links entirely — Google's own
Discover feature now layers AI summaries
on top of publisher content, threatening the traffic publishers built their business models around. The Buffer completes what AI search started. SEO was always a proxy metric — you optimized for signals that correlated with quality because quality itself was hard to measure directly. The Buffer measures quality directly. It does not care about keyword density, internal linking, or domain authority. It cares about whether the content, when stripped to its core claim, is true and relevant to the person it's serving. What replaces SEO is something harder to game: content written with machine-parseable structure as an explicit design goal. Clear claims. Explicit evidence. Named conclusions. Timestamped data. The conventions that academic writing developed for making arguments legible to expert readers will migrate into journalism and long-form content — not in style, but in epistemic discipline. Not because editors mandate it, but because content lacking these properties gets compressed into something so thin it loses meaning.
Social content faces a different problem. Tweets, LinkedIn posts, short-form video — the social stack — was built on the premise that the emotional hit and the information are the same thing. The format collapses when the Buffer strips the emotional packaging and hands you the underlying proposition, which is usually either obvious or empty. The formats that survive are those where the human experience of encountering the content is itself the content: the comedian's timing, the athlete's performance, the artist's process caught live. What doesn't survive is the hot take, the viral opinion formatted as a list, the market commentary that is really just confident-sounding noise dressed up as analysis. The Buffer will drain the social graph of a specific kind of engagement — the kind that was always about the feeling of being informed, rather than being informed.
Newsletters face a bifurcation. The paid newsletter model — built on the proposition that one smart person's synthesis is worth paying for — has a complicated future. On one hand, the Buffer can replicate the synthesis function for generic curation newsletters automatically, eating the floor of the market. On the other hand, newsletters where the human voice, the specific relationships, the insider access, and the aesthetic sensibility are the product — where you are paying for the person, not the information — become more valuable. The writer who survives is the writer who has something that cannot be prompted out of a model: a perspective built from a specific life, access that doesn't exist in any training set, a relationship with readers that is fundamentally social rather than informational.
A premium on irreducibility. The deepest consequence is the hardest to predict: content that cannot be adequately captured in a Buffer summary becomes scarce and therefore valuable. Literary journalism that earns its length. Reported narrative that depends on detail and accumulation. Live performance. Conversation. The human artifacts that lose most of their value when summarized will become the artifacts people seek out precisely because the Buffer handles everything else. We already see this dynamic with physical books in the streaming era — the format that was supposed to die became a cultural status object. Long-form writing, live events, and in-person experience may undergo the same premiumization in the Buffer era, not because they are inherently superior, but because they cannot be intermediated away.
In short: the Buffer bifurcates the content market at every level. Informational content gets compressed, intermediated, and commoditized. Experiential and irreducibly human content gets premiumized. The middle — competent, generic, neither dense enough to survive compression nor human enough to resist it — gets hollowed out. For creators, the question is brutally simple: are you producing signal, or are you producing noise that has learned to look like signal? The Buffer will tell the difference, and it will not be polite about it.

The Political Economy of the Buffer

The Buffer sounds benevolent until you ask who builds it.
If the Buffer is open-source and locally deployed — the personal AI server architecture — the answer is: you do, or your community does, and the configuration is transparent and auditable. The coherence it produces is your coherence, built from your values.
If the Buffer is a feature of a large platform — Apple Intelligence summarizing your notifications, Google's AI deciding what emails matter, Meta's algorithm deciding which posts from your friends to surface — then the platform controls the synthesis layer. And this is where the political economy of the Buffer gets genuinely dangerous: controlling synthesis is more powerful than controlling distribution, by a wide margin. You can route around a platform's distribution chokepoints by publishing elsewhere. A blog, a newsletter, an RSS feed — the decentralized web still exists and content that gets blocked in one place can surface in another. There is no equivalent escape from a platform's Buffer. If Apple Intelligence decides your newsletter doesn't warrant a notification, it never reaches the reader. If Google's AI decides your email is low-priority, it stays buried. You cannot publish around a synthesis layer. You can only build your own.
The geopolitical dimension sharpens this further. When DeepSeek, the Chinese AI model, launched in early 2025, researchers immediately documented
systematic censorship
baked into its outputs — topics like Taiwan and Tiananmen Square filtered at the synthesis layer, invisibly, before reaching the user. That is what a state-controlled Buffer looks like. The mechanism is identical whether the actor is a government or a corporation optimizing for quarterly revenue. The synthesis layer encodes values. The question is always whose.
The network effect of the Buffer compounds this asymmetry. A Buffer trained on more of your data, connected to more of your tools, running more continuously in the background, becomes increasingly accurate and increasingly indispensable. The switching cost is not learning a new interface — it is losing a model that has built a year's worth of context about how you think, what you care about, and what you need to know. That context is the moat. Whoever captures that relationship early captures it deeply, and the cost of moving to a competitor rises with every week the Buffer runs.
This is the structural reason why the open-model path matters. The Buffer will exist regardless — the gap between information production and human processing capacity is too large to leave unfilled. The question is whether the layer that fills it is configured by you or by a platform optimizing for its own quarterly numbers.
There is a deeper economic consequence that hasn't been fully reckoned with. The entire attention economy — the $600 billion advertising market built on interrupting people — is predicated on direct access to human attention. The Buffer removes that access. If your persistent AI intermediary pre-filters everything before it reaches you, the interruptible moment that advertising depends on disappears. You cannot serve an ad to someone whose Buffer decided the ad wasn't worth surfacing. You cannot run a retargeting campaign against someone whose attention is pre-allocated before any platform sees it. The Buffer is, structurally, an attention union — a permanent gatekeeper that negotiates on your behalf what gets through. This does not merely disrupt advertising. It makes the business model of interruption architecturally impossible at scale. The platforms that survive will be the ones that figure out how to be worth surfacing, not how to force their way in.

The Buffer Is Already Here

The honest observation is that the Buffer is not a future artifact. It already exists in early form everywhere: in the
"Summaries" tab in Gmail
, in
Claude
and
ChatGPT
's document synthesis, in
Notion AI
's "Summarize this page," in
Apple's notification digests
, in the "Catch me up" features rolling out across every major platform. A startup called
Noscroll
launched in 2026 with a single premise: an AI bot that browses your social feeds and texts you when something important happens. "No feed. No brainrot." That is the Buffer reduced to a product pitch.
What does not exist yet is the integration layer — a Buffer that holds coherent context across your email, your feeds, your documents, your calendar, your conversations, and synthesizes across all of them continuously rather than addressing each in isolation. That is the hard problem, and it is a hard problem that is being actively worked on in every major AI lab and dozens of open-source projects.
When it gets solved, the experience of being informed will change as fundamentally as it changed when the internet moved from directories to search. Before search, you navigated. After search, you queried. Before the Buffer, you consumed. After the Buffer, you receive a synthesis.
The question worth sitting with is not whether this happens but what kind of intelligence you want doing the synthesizing — and whether, when the Buffer arrives at scale, you will have had any say in how it was built.

The Coherence Dividend

There is a version of this future that is genuinely good. A world where every person has access to a well-configured Buffer is a world where the advantage of the well-resourced analyst — who has staff to read and synthesize on their behalf — is distributed to everyone. Where the cognitive overhead of staying informed drops dramatically. Where the information asymmetries that currently advantage institutional actors over individuals start to close.
The analyst at a hedge fund does not read every filing — they have a team that reads and summarizes. The busy executive does not track every relevant development — they have an EA who does. The Buffer is the democratization of that function. And the coherence it produces is not just a personal luxury; it is a precondition for the kind of informed deliberation that good collective decisions require.
The real stakes of the Buffer are not technical. They are about authorship. Right now, you are not the author of your information environment — platforms are. The Buffer, built right, hands that authorship back. Built wrong, it just changes which corporation writes the narrative you live inside.
The Buffer is coming. The only question is whether you'll be the one holding the pen.
One last observation worth sitting with: Tim Berners-Lee spent two decades trying to build a machine-readable web — a semantic layer on top of the internet where content would be tagged, structured, and legible to automated reasoning. It failed because humans don't produce machine-readable content naturally; the friction of structured markup was too high and the benefits too diffuse. The Buffer solves the same problem from the other direction. Instead of making content machine-readable at the point of production, you put a machine between the content and the human at the point of consumption. The infrastructure Berners-Lee imagined arrives not through markup or metadata standards but through intermediation. The semantic web doesn't get built. It gets inferred, continuously, by every Buffer running against the existing web. The dream of a machine-legible information layer is being realized — just not the way anyone predicted.
